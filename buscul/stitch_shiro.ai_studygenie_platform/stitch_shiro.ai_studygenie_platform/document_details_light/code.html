The Mechanism of Oxidative Phosphorylation

schedule 24 Min Read
calendar_today Oct 24, 2023
Peer Reviewed

Oxidative phosphorylation is the metabolic pathway in which cells use enzymes to oxidize nutrients, thereby releasing energy which is used to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In most eukaryotes, this takes place inside mitochondria.

Electron Transport Chain Overview

Almost all aerobic organisms carry out oxidative phosphorylation. This pathway is probably so pervasive because it is a highly efficient way of releasing energy, compared to alternative fermentation processes such as anaerobic glycolysis.

During oxidative phosphorylation, electrons are transferred from electron donors to electron acceptors such as oxygen in redox reactions. These redox reactions release energy, which is used to form ATP. In eukaryotes, these redox reactions are catalyzed by a series of protein complexes within the inner membrane of the cell's mitochondria.

Complex I (NADH coenzyme Q reductase)

Complex I is the first enzyme of the respiratory chain. It is a huge protein, containing 45 amino acid chains in mammals. It catalyzes the transfer of two electrons from NADH to coenzyme Q10 (ubiquinone), a lipid-soluble carrier dissolved in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Key Takeaway

The primary function of Complex I is to pump four protons across the inner membrane for every pair of electrons transferred, contributing significantly to the electrochemical gradient essential for ATP synthesis.

The energy released by this electron transfer is used to translocate protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane. This creates an electrochemical gradient that is subsequently utilized by ATP synthase to generate ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate.
